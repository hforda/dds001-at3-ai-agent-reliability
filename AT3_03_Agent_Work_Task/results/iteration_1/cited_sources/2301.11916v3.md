[s0] [ [ January 14, 2024 ==================== In recent years, pre-trained large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable efficiency in achieving an inference-time few-shot learning capability known as in-context learning.

[s1] However, existing literature has highlighted the sensitivity of this capability to the selection of few-shot demonstrations.

[s2] Current understandings of the underlying mechanisms by which this capability arises from regular language model pretraining objectives remain disconnected from the real-world LLMs.

[s3] This study aims to examine the in-context learning phenomenon through a Bayesian lens, viewing real-world LLMs as latent variable models.

[s4] On this premise, we propose an algorithm to select optimal demonstrations from a set of annotated data with a small LM, and then directly generalize the selected demonstrations to larger LMs.

[s5] We demonstrate significant improvement over baselines, averaged over eight GPT models on eight real-world text classification datasets.

[s6] We also demonstrate the real-world usefulness of our algorithm on GSM8K, a math word problem dataset.

[s7] Our empirical findings support our hypothesis that LLMs implicitly infer a latent variable containing task information.

[s8] [Code: <https://github.com/WANGXinyiLinda/concept-based-demonstration-selection>.] § INTRODUCTION Transformer-based <cit.> pre-trained large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated significant advancements in a variety of natural language processing (NLP) tasks.

[s9] As the size of these LLMs increases, they gain “in-context learning” capabilities, whereby the models achieve state-of-the-art (SOTA) or near-SOTA performance by conditioning on a small number of demonstration examples at inference time, without any need for updating model parameters <cit.>.  ← **本簡報引用此句**

[s10] Below is an example input sequence for semantic analysis with in-context learning: The first two lines are two demonstrations, and the third line is a test input.

[s11] We expect an LLM to output the correct label as a continuation.

[s12] In-context learning has been demonstrated to be an effective technique for a wide range of NLP tasks.

[s13] However, it is sensitive to the choice, format, and even the order of the demonstrations used <cit.>.

[s14] This makes achieving optimal performance with in-context learning a significant challenge, requiring real human effort to adjust the format and selection of demonstration examples.

[s15] Heuristic solutions, such as selecting demonstrations based on the similarity between the demonstrations and test input <cit.> have been proposed, but a comprehensive understanding of why certain demonstrations are effective while others are not remains elusive.

[s16] Additionally, the mechanisms by which LLMs acquire in-context learning capabilities through training on natural text under the standard language model pre-training objective are not fully understood.

[s17] Recent works on understanding in-context learning provide valuable insights and theoretical results <cit.>, but are limited in scope, focusing on synthetic experiments to validate their hypotheses, while it remains unclear if these results generalize to LLMs pre-trained on real-world natural language data.

[s18] <cit.> introduced a prominent result providing a latent topic (concept) variable interpretation for in-context learning.

[s19] They showed that the in-context learning predictor approaches the Bayes optimal predictor when the number of demonstrations approaches infinity, under the assumption that both the pre-training data distribution and task-specific data distribution are Hidden Markov Models (HMM).

[s20] However, the assumption that the data generation process is Hidden Markovian makes extrapolation of the result to natural language questionable, and restricts empirical verification to synthetic data with toy models.

[s21] We are inspired by this prior work and introduce a more general and natural explanation built on realistic assumptions, which gives rise to a practical demonstration selection algorithm.

[s22] Our explanation is inspired by the generation process of a topic model, i.e.

[s23] a simple latent variable model: P(w_1:T) = ∫_Θ P(w_1:T|θ) P(θ) dθ Where θ∈Θ represents a potentially high dimensional topic/concept variable, Θ is the space of the topic/concept variable, and w_1:T refers to the token sequence of a piece of text.

[s24] Note that the topic model here refers to the modern neural topic models <cit.>.

[s25] On the other hand, generative LLMs model text data according to the general probabilistic decomposition: P(w_1:T) = ∏_i=1^T P(w_i|w_i-1,...,w_1) While in practice, LLMs generate new tokens based on all previous tokens, we investigate whether a simplified assumption similar to that of topic models can be made for LLMs: P_M(w_t+1:T|w_1:t) = ∫_Θ P_M(w_t+1:T|θ) P_M(θ|w_1:t) dθ In this scenario,

[s26] the generated tokens are assumed to be conditionally independent of previous tokens, given the latent topic (concept) variable that acts like an approximate sufficient statistic for the posterior information related to the prompt w_1:t.

[s27] For in-context learning, this concept variable includes format and task information.

[s28] By conditioning on an appropriate latent concept variable, LLMs would generate the desired continuation with P(w_t+1:T|θ).

[s29] As LLMs do not explicitly learn a latent variable distribution like LDA-style topic models <cit.>, we can instead utilize this formulation under an Empirical Bayesian formulation inspired by <cit.> to only approximate the optimal latent variable value for a desired task, using a small LLM (with less than 1B parameters), which is computationally efficient.

[s30] We empirically validate our explanation by selecting examples (w_1:t in the equations) that are most likely to infer the optimal latent variable value (those with the highest posterior probability P(θ|w_t+1:T)).

[s31] We then directly use them as demonstrations for in-context learning with other larger LLMs (up to 175B parameters) and observed a significant performance improvement.

[s32] The generalization of demonstrations between LLMs is likely a result of similar pre-training data distributions.

[s33] While our work is inspired by that of <cit.>, our approach differs significantly in both theoretical analysis and experimental settings.

[s34] Our main contributions are as follows: * We assume a general data generation process specified by a three-variable causal graph, without constraints on the distribution function or the number of demonstrations.

[s35] * We prove under these realistic assumptions that the in-context learning predictor can reach the Bayes optimal predictor with a finite number of demonstrations chosen using the latent concept variable.

[s36] * We introduce an efficient, practical demonstration selection algorithm based on our theoretical results, which can select demonstrations using a small LLM and then directly generalize the demonstrations to other LLMs.

[s37] The effectiveness of our algorithm is empirically validated using real-world LLMs on both text classification tasks and math word problems.

[s38] Our goal is to close the gap between theoretical understandings and real-world LLMs.

[s39] To the best of our knowledge, our proposed latent variable explanation of in-context learning is the first Bayesian explanation that yields an effective algorithm in real-world scenarios.

[s40] § THEORETICAL ANALYSIS In in-context learning, the prompt w_1:t is composed of several demonstrations and a test input.

[s41] The generated tokens w_t+1:T represent the model's prediction for the test input.

[s42] §.§ Notations and Problem Setting Suppose the objective of our task is to predict a discrete target variable Y ∈𝒴, given a token sequence X ∈𝒳, where 𝒳 is the space of all possible token sequences.

[s43] θ∈Θ is a potentially high dimensional latent variable, where Θ is the high dimensional space of the variable.

[s44] Unlike the traditional topic model, θ is not assumed to be discrete, but continuously distributed over Θ.

[s45] To define the data generation process, we posit the existence of an underlying causal relation between X, Y, and θ.

[s46] We examine two potential directions of this causal relation, namely X Y θ and Y X θ, which can be represented mathematically as the following structural equations: Y = f(X, θ, ϵ) X = g(Y, θ, ϵ) Here ϵ∈ℰ is an independent noise variable, f: 𝒳×Θ×ℰ→𝒴 and g: 𝒴×Θ×ℰ→𝒳 are two deterministic functions.

[s47] Furthermore, we denote the joint data distribution by X, Y, θ∼ P, and assume that Y is sampled from a uniform distribution over 𝒴.

[s48] The distinction between these two directions is crucial, as it allows us to utilize the direction in which the child variable (Y or X) is independent of the other variables, given its parents.

[s49] We hypothesize that the causal direction depends on the nature of the task.

[s50] For instance, in the task of predicting the sentiment (Y) of a movie review (X), it is reasonable to assume that the opinion about the movie is formed before writing the review, thus making Y the cause of X, along with the task concept of “writing a passage to express one's opinion about the movie" (θ).

[s51] Conversely, for the task of classifying whether a product review (X) is helpful to other customers (Y), it is the quality of the review (X) that cause other customers to upvote it (Y), along with the task concept of “rating the helpfulness of this review" (θ).

[s52] In the rest of the paper, we will focus on the X Y θ direction and leave a detailed discussion of the other direction in the Appendix.

[s53] Suppose we are interested in a task (e.g.

[s54] semantic analysis) denoted by d ∈𝒯, where 𝒯 is the space of all possible tasks.

[s55] We assume there is an injective function between 𝒯 and Θ.

[s56] i.e.

[s57] for each task d, there is a concept variable θ^d, such that each data (X^d, Y^d) sampled from task d is generated by: Y^d = f(X^d, θ^d, ϵ) To perform in-context learning with an LLM (generically denoted by model label M), we condition on a fixed set of k demonstration examples (X^d_1, Y^d_1), (X^d_2, Y^d_2), ..., (X^d_k, Y^d_k) sampled from task d.

[s58] Following previous works <cit.>, as we are not using any instruction fine-tuned models, we do not include a task description in the prompt, with the aim of focusing on the examination of the demonstrations.

[s59] To naturally project 𝒴 into the token space 𝒳, we define injective mappings τ^d: 𝒴→𝒳, which are typically defined by human understanding of the task d.

[s60] e.g.

[s61] for sentiment analysis, τ^d map positive class to the token “positive" and negative class to the token “negative".

[s62] Additionally, a delimiter token w^d is defined, typically an empty space or a new line token, to separate the demonstrations when concatenated.

[s63] We denote the LLM output probability of X, Y, and θ, with the aforementioned preprocessing applied, by P_M^d: P_M(τ^d(Y)|X^d_1, τ^d(Y^d_1), w^d, ..., X^d_k, τ^d(Y^d_k), w^d, X) =P_M^d(Y|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k, X) §.§ Problem Analysis and Theoretical Results Suppose a set of observed data sampled from task d, denoted as 𝒟^d, is available, allowing for the selection of the k most suitable demonstrations from it.

[s64] For any incoming test example X, we have: P_M^d(Y|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k, X) = ∫_Θ P_M^d(Y|θ, X) P_M^d(θ|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k, X) dθ Here, we assume the sampling of the test example is independent of the sampling of the demonstrations, so Y is independent of the demonstrations given θ and X.

[s65] We also assume that the pre-trained data distribution P_M^d is a suitable approximation of the assumed data distribution P: Assume that P_M(X) = P(X), and P_M^d(Y|θ, X) ∝ P(Y|θ, X) for X Y θ.

[s66] Note that the assumption that a large language model captures the true distribution of language is fairly common in the literature studying LLMs <cit.>.

[s67] With this assumption, we establish: If task d follows the X Y θ direction, then _y∈𝒴 P_M^d(Y=y|θ^d, X) is the Bayes optimal classifier.

[s68] In this case, only when P_M^d(θ|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k, X) completely concentrate on θ^d, can the in-context learning classifier become the Bayes optimal classifier <cit.>: If task d follows the X Y θ direction, then the in-context learning classifier _y∈𝒴P_M^d(Y=y|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k, X) always has a higher or equal probability of misclassification to the Bayes optimal classifier _y∈𝒴 P_M^d(Y=y|θ^d, X).

[s69] Equality only holds when ∀ x ∈𝒳, P_M^d(θ^d|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k, X=x)=1.

[s70] A similar argument can be made for the Y X θ direction.

[s71] [The detailed argument of the Y X θ direction can be found in <ref>.] Here, <ref> would become: P_M^d(X|Y^d_1, X^d_1, ..., Y^d_k, X^d_k, Y) = ∫_Θ P_M^d(X|θ, Y) P_M^d(θ|Y^d_1, X^d_1, ..., Y^d_k, X^d_k, Y) dθ Note that the left-hand side of <ref> and <ref> are similar to the direct and channel method introduced by <cit.>.

[s72] However, our analysis differs from theirs in that we do not treat (Y X θ) as the universally superior channel direction for modeling in-context learning, rather arguing that depending on the end task, the causal direction (X Y θ) is sometimes better.

[s73] This view is supported by our empirical results in <ref>.

[s74] § METHOD Here we demonstrate how the proposed theory can be practically applied to select optimal demonstration examples.

[s75] Since latent variable θ encodes both the task and format information, the whole distribution over Θ is too complex to model.

[s76] Unlike traditional topic models, we will only focus on estimating an optimal value θ^d corresponding to task d.

[s77] First, we perform latent concept learning, wherein the task latent θ^d is learned as a set of new token embeddings using prompt tuning over the full demonstration candidate set.

[s78] With this optimal task latent, we then perform demonstration selection, where a smaller set of demonstrations is chosen to maximize the likelihood of postfixing the latent concept tokens.

[s79] We only need to use a small LLM to do the above steps to obtain an optimal set of demonstrations that can be directly transferred to other LLMs.

[s80] <ref> is an overall illustration of our proposed method.

[s81] §.§ Latent Concept Learning We want to first find the optimal value of the latent concept variable θ^d corresponding to a task d ∈𝒯.

[s82] As _y∈𝒴 P_M^d(Y=y|θ^d, X) is the Bayes optimal classifier according to <ref>, θ^d should be able to minimize -𝔼_X,Y,d[logP_M^d(Y|θ^d, X)] for the X Y θ direction.

[s83] In practice, we try to align θ^d to the token embedding space by adding new tokens to the vocabulary.

[s84] After this alignment, we hope to be able to use the learned new tokens of θ^d as regular tokens.

[s85] More specifically, building upon the methodology proposed by <cit.>, for each specific task d, c new concept tokens (denoted as θ̂^d) are added to the original vocabulary of LLM M to represent the corresponding task concept θ^d.

[s86] Subsequently, the embedding of these new tokens E_new(θ̂^d) is fine-tuned while freezing the remaining parameters of LLM M.

[s87] The variable c is treated as a hyperparameter.

[s88] In practice, in order to condition on θ^d, the corresponding c concept tokens are appended to the input X (or Y) as shown in the example provided below, where c=2: By giving the above input tokens, we ask the LLM to predict the correct label for us.

[s89] Note that here is just a label assigned to the newly added concept token.

[s90] It can be anything as long as it does not overlap with the original vocabulary of LLM.

[s91] The fine-tuning objective would then be minimizing ℒ(θ̂^d) = 𝔼_X,Y[ℓ(X,Y;θ̂^d)], where ℓ(X,Y;θ̂^d) = -logP_M^d(Y|θ̂^d, X) if X Y θ -logP_M^d(X|θ̂^d, Y) if Y X θ.

[s92] Theoretically, if we can minimize the above loss function, a Bayes optimal classifier can be obtained, and the concept tokens would be a reasonable delegate of the real latent concept variable: When ℒ(θ̂^d) is minimized, P_M^d(Y|θ̂^d, X) = P(Y|θ^d, X) for X Y θ.

[s93] If the LLM M is invertible, then θ̂^d = θ^d.[More discussion can be found in <ref>.] We denote the LLM M with fine-tuned concept tokens by M'.

[s94] Since we add the concept tokens into the regular token vocabulary, the raw LLM output probability P_M'(θ̂^d|w_1:t) (w_1:t denote a given prompt) would be in the token sequence space 𝒳 instead of the concept space Θ.

[s95] Since learning all possible θ^d ∈Θ is infeasible, we propose to approximate the concept space Θ by sampling a diverse subset of tasks 𝒮⊆𝒯.

[s96] Then the estimated conditional probability of θ^d would be: P̂_M'^d(θ̂^d|w_1:t) = P_M'^d(θ̂^d|w_1:t)/∑_t ∈𝒮P_M'^t(θ̂^t|w_1:t) To obtain the concept tokens for all tasks in 𝒮, we fine-tune all tasks together with the loss ∑_d∈𝒮ℒ(θ^d).

[s97] We summarize the proposed algorithm in <ref>.

[s98] Note that the embedding matrix of a generative LLM is shared on both the input and output sides.

[s99] So while we only see the concept tokens on the input side at the training time, they can be viewed as regular word tokens that can be generated on the output side.

[s100] §.§ Demonstration Selection According to <ref>, for a task d, to make the in-context learning classifier closer to the Bayes optimal classifier, we need to select demonstrations (X^d_1, Y^d_1), ..., (X^d_k, Y^d_k) that maximize P_M^d(θ^d|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k, X) for all X ∈𝒳.

[s101] Then our goal then becomes selecting demonstrations that can best infer the task concept for all test inputs on average: _X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k𝔼_X[P_M^d(θ^d|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k, X)] As test examples are sampled independent of the demonstrations, and P_M(X) = P(X) according to <ref>, we have 𝔼_X[P_M^d(θ^d|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k, X)] = P_M^d(θ^d|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ...,

[s102] X^d_k, Y^d_k) If we assume each demonstration is also sampled independently, we have: P_M^d(θ^d|X^d_1, Y^d_1, ..., X^d_k, Y^d_k) = ∏_i=1^k P_M^d(θ^d|X_i^d, Y_i^d)/P_M^d(θ^d)^k-1 Assuming that θ has a uniform prior, then our goal becomes finding the top k demonstrations that maximize P̂_M'^d(θ̂^d|X_i^d, Y_i^d).

[s103] Note that the independence between demonstrations is a simplified assumption to reduce the combinatory search space of (X^d_1, Y^d_1), ..., (X^d_k, Y^d_k).

[s104] In practice, selected demonstrations are likely correlated as some demonstrations may work well together but not necessarily work well by themselves.

[s105] However, it would be too expensive to search the O(|𝒟^d|^k) combinations over the candidate set 𝒟^d.

[s106] In practice, this simplification works reasonably well.

[s107] We leave this combinatory search problem to future research.

[s108] Also, as we are using an LLM to approximate the data distribution, the order of the demonstrations might matter.

[s109] We will show in the Experiment section that the order does not matter, so no reordering of the selected demonstrations is needed.

[s110] The full selection algorithm is shown in <ref>.

[s111] § EXPERIMENTS Datasets.

[s112] We conduct experiments on eight datasets from five different types of NLP classification tasks: sentiment analysis, linguistic analysis, topic classification, emotion classification, and hate speech detection.

[s113] For sentiment analysis, we choose the Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST2) dataset <cit.> from the GLUE benchmark <cit.> and the financial phrase bank (FPB) dataset <cit.>.

[s114] SST2 is constructed based on movie reviews labeled “positive" or “negative", and FPB is based on financial news labeled “positive", “negative", or “neutral".

[s115] For linguistic analysis, we choose the Corpus of Linguistic Acceptability (COLA) dataset <cit.> from the GLUE benchmark, based on sentences collected from linguistic books, labeled with “acceptable" or “unacceptable".

[s116] For topic classification, we choose the DBpedia ontology classification dataset <cit.>, based on DBpedia 2014 <cit.>, labeled with 14 different ontology classes.

[s117] For emotion classification, we choose the dataset from <cit.> and <cit.>, both of which are collected from Twitter.

[s118] <cit.> (EmoC) predict emotion given a three-turn contextual dialogue, while <cit.> predict emotion given a Twitter message with clear emotion.

[s119] For hate speech detection, we choose the online hate speech detection dataset (ETHOS) <cit.>, collected from online social media platforms.

[s120] Here we detect two types of hate speech: sexual orientation (ETHOS-SO) and religion (ETHOS-R).

[s121] While in <ref>, we assume that all tasks share the same label space 𝒴, here we relax such assumption and allow a different number of labels for different tasks.

[s122] We use minimal formatting to process each example.

[s123] A detailed description of the datasets and our data processing procedure can be found in <ref>.

[s124] Experiment settings.

[s125] To determine the causal direction for each task, we select the direction that can give higher accuracy when using random demonstrations[Detailed results see <ref> in <ref>.].

[s126] We adopt the Y → X ←θ direction for sentiment analysis, topic classification, and emotion classification tasks, which is consistent with the intuition that people usually have some sentiment, topic, or emotion in mind before writing a piece of text.

[s127] We adopt the X → Y ←θ direction for the linguistic analysis and hate speech detection type of tasks.

[s128] While this is less intuitive, we can understand this as linguistic error and hate speech detection are more of a post hoc task in contrast to the previous tasks.

[s129] Without specification, we use k=4 number of demonstrations and c=10 number of concept tokens per dataset for our experiments, as the context length of GPT2 is 1024, and a larger number of demonstrations may not be able to completely fit into it.

[s130] We use GPT2-large to learn the concept tokens and then compute the probability of each candidate demonstration example.

[s131] We select our demonstrations from a randomly selected 100 example subset of the train set as the candidate set 𝒟^d.

[s132] We use the same set of demonstrations selected by GPT2-large for all other LLMs.

[s133] We test the performance of the selected demonstrations using at most 1000 examples randomly sampled from the test set.

[s134] Each experiment is repeated for five runs with different random seeds (the randomness comes from the sampling of the candidate set and the sampling of the test set).

[s135] We adopt a large portion of the code from <cit.>, which is based on Huggingface <cit.>.

[s136] Baselines.

[s137] We consider the following baselines: * Uniform: We uniformly select k demonstrations from 𝒟 for each test example.

[s138] * Similar: According to <cit.>, demonstrations that are semantically similar to the test example would hare more performant.

[s139] Following their method, we use a pre-trained sentence Transformer <cit.> to calculate the cosine similarity between the demonstrations and test examples.

[s140] We choose the top k similar demonstrations from 𝒟 for each test example.

[s181] The longer the concept token sequence is, the more likely it will contain meaningless tokens that do not contribute to demonstration selection.

[s182] Effect of demonstrations' order.

[s183] We find that the demonstrations selected by our method are insensitive to their order in most cases.[Detailed results see <ref> in <ref>.] An exception is the EmoC dataset, where our method has a high variance.

[s184] On the contrary, <cit.> found that the order of the demonstration matters, and a good ordering cannot be transferred between different LLMs.

[s185] We suspect that the ordering only matters when the demonstration selection method is not robust.

[s186] Since <cit.> randomly selects one set of demonstrations for the whole test set, the variance in performance is high with different demonstrations, thus ordering matters.

[s187] And since such ordering is not transferable while our selected demonstrations are highly transferable, we suspect the core task information is stored in the content of the demonstrations, while the ordering mainly captures model-specific artifacts.

[s201] § RELATED WORK Heuristic solutions, such as selecting demonstrations based on the similarity between the demonstrations and test input <cit.> have been proposed.

[s202] <cit.> propose to reorder the demonstration based on the entropy of the predicted labels.

[s203] In this paper, we use the similarity-based selection method as a baseline while do not include the label entropy-based reordering method as we show that the ordering of the demonstrations does not matter for our method.

[s204] Previous research on the phenomenon of in-context learning in Transformers has identified a number of pre-training data distributions that can lead to the emergence of this capability, including a Hidden Markov Model distribution <cit.> and a skewed Zipfian distribution with high burstiness <cit.>.

[s205] Other studies have sought to understand the underlying mechanisms of in-context learning by making connections with gradient descent <cit.>, formalizing it as an algorithm learning problem <cit.>, or proposing a latent variable theory similar as ours <cit.>.

[s206] While providing valuable insights on how in-context learning works, these works are limited to synthetic datasets and toy Transformers, while it remains unclear if these results generalize to LLMs pre-trained on real-world text data and whether these results can help in-context learning performance.

[s207] In contrast, we propose a Bayesian explanation of in-context learning that can be verified with real-world LLMs on various NLP datasets.

[s208] Dai et al.

[s209] <cit.> provide a practical algorithm based on the understanding that the Transformer has a dual form of gradient descent.

[s210] However, their empirical results are smaller in scale, with six datasets and only one model (350M), and has less significant improvements (5.4% relative to baseline).

[s211] There are also works trying to understand in-context learning from an empirical perspective <cit.>.

[s212] Min et al.

[s213] <cit.> found demonstrations' ground truth labels do not matter for in-context learning, which we find is not entirely accurate in <ref>.

[s214] On the other hand, chain-of-thoughts prompting <cit.> find that providing step-by-step explanations improves in-context learning performance.

[s215] § CONCLUSION In this work, we endeavor to comprehend large language models (LLMs) through a Bayesian lens and posit them as implicit topic models that infer a latent conceptual variable from prompts.

[s216] Motivated by this understanding, we propose a two-step algorithm that first extracts latent conceptual tokens from a small LLM and then selects demonstrations that have the greatest probability of predicting the corresponding conceptual tokens.

[s217] The selected demonstrations can then be directly generalized to other LLMs.

[s218] The efficacy of our algorithm across various text classification datasets and GPT models validates our explanation of in-context learning.
